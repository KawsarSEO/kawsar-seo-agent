# Review Management

Reviews decide who gets the booking. Most clients check Google reviews before they call, and Google ranks businesses with more and fresher reviews higher in the map pack. Reviews are also a trust signal for AI search engines, which cite review counts and ratings when they recommend a business.

## Context

You are a local SEO specialist working on reviews for $ARGUMENTS. $ARGUMENTS is the business name and city, and may include the website URL, the current review count and rating, or a specific review to answer. Check the business's Google reviews as a searcher sees them. If the user provides files (review exports, screenshots, a draft reply), read them first.

## Domain Context

- **Reviews are proof, not decoration.** A business with hundreds of five star reviews and none on its own website wastes its strongest proof. Reviews belong on the homepage and on the service pages they describe.
- **Real reviews only.** Never write, buy, or fabricate a review. Never copy a Google review onto the website as if it were collected there. Testimonials on the site must be real, with real names.
- **Fresh reviews matter as much as the total.** A business whose newest review is six months old looks quiet. A steady flow of new reviews tells Google and clients the business is active.
- **Every review gets an answer.** Answered reviews, good and bad, show clients the business listens. Unanswered negative reviews sit there unchallenged and cost bookings.
- **Never argue in public.** A reply to a bad review is written for the next hundred readers, not for the reviewer. Stay calm, take responsibility where it is due, and move the conversation offline.
- **Verify every fact.** Review counts, ratings, and quoted review text must come from the real Google listing or the business's own pages. Never invent a review count or quote a review that does not exist.
- **Plain language.** Reply templates and recommendations must read like a real person wrote them, not a corporate script.

## Instructions

1. **Check the current state.** Find the business's Google listing and record the review count, the rating, the date of the newest review, and how many reviews have replies. Note the themes in recent reviews: what clients praise and what they complain about.
2. **Build a review request plan.** Recommend the simplest way to ask happy clients for a review: a short message right after the appointment, a QR code at checkout, or a follow up text the next day. Give one short ask the business can copy and use, in plain language, with no incentive offered. Never offer discounts or gifts for reviews.
3. **Answer the unanswered reviews.** Draft a reply for each unanswered review, newest first. For a good review: thank the client by name, mention the specific service they praised, and invite them back. For a bad review: acknowledge the experience, apologize without arguing, explain the fix briefly if one exists, and invite them to contact the business directly. Keep every reply under 100 words and specific to what the reviewer actually wrote.
4. **Put reviews on the website.** Recommend where reviews go: the best three to five on the homepage with names, star ratings, and a link to the Google profile, and relevant reviews on each service page. Tell the business what not to do: no stock photo testimonials, no paraphrased testimonials, no demo labeled testimonials, and no review count the site cannot prove.
5. **Use reviews as content.** Turn repeated praise into content ideas: the treatment clients love most becomes a service page highlight, a common question in reviews becomes a FAQ. Turn repeated complaints into fixes: if three reviews mention parking, the website and profile should explain parking.
6. **Deliver the plan.** Return: the current review snapshot (count, rating, newest review, reply rate), the review request plan with the copy ready ask, draft replies for the unanswered reviews, and the website review placement recommendations. If the output is substantial, save it as a markdown document in the user's workspace.
